## A. Evidence

### A1. What predicts and improves drawing accuracy

Sources of error.
- Cohen and Bennett (1997) proposed four sources of drawing error: misperception of the object, misperception of the drawing, motor skill, and representational decisions. They compared tracing a photograph, drawing a photograph, drawing a tracing and tracing a tracing. The largest errors came in the drawing conditions, so they concluded that misperceiving the object is the main source of error. Motor coordination was a very small source, decisions a minor one, and in their data misperceiving one's own drawing was not a source at all. (Citation details confirmed in search results; findings as summarised in Chamberlain and Wagemans's preprint and the Ostrofsky 2015 paper, both surfaced by search.)
- Later work qualifies the last point. Ostrofsky, Kozbelt and Cohen (2015), as returned by search, note that most researchers conflate misperception of the drawing with misperception of the object. They cite Perdreau and Cavanagh (2015), who found that people who could detect changes in both their drawing and the model went on to draw more accurately. Perdreau and Cavanagh's abstract reports that experts had better memory than novices for both the original and their own drawing.
- Chamberlain and Wagemans (2016), a review in Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews, conclude that accurate perception of the subject and of the drawing is at the heart of drawing skill, though drawing skill does not produce fundamental changes in visual perception. Representational decisions, such as how the scene is segmented and the pull of learned schemas, play a decisive role. The authors say flexible, domain-specific, top-down control of visual attention is critical. (Abstract and repository records found by search.)

How skilled drawers look and move.
- Cohen (2005), "Look little, look often" (Empirical Studies of the Arts), defined gaze frequency as how often the artist glances between drawing and subject. Papers citing it report that more frequent switching goes with more accurate drawings and less reliance on memory. A UCL study of copying a house figure cites Cohen for the view that accuracy is best when attention switches often and focuses on a small area of the model. I did not see the original statistics.
- Tchalenko (2009, Vision Research) recorded 16 people from novice to expert copying a line drawing of a figure. Experts copied accurately while all beginners made marked errors of scale, proportion and shape. Only experts broke the original into simple line sections and copied them one at a time, using a direct eye-hand strategy that did not need the shape held in visual memory first. Later summaries describe experts as pausing more often and drawing shorter segments.
- Tchalenko and Miall (2009, Cortex) tested ten beginner art students under four copying conditions, including blind copying and copying from memory. In direct copying, shape could be drawn accurately without seeing the drawing hand or the paper. The eye alternated between the original (for shape) and the copy (for position) "just in time".
- Perdreau and Cavanagh (2014, i-Perception) found that skilled drawers encoded object structure faster and could classify structure at smaller sizes in peripheral vision. The authors link drawing accuracy to faster encoding of object structure and better access to crowded detail.

Individual differences and practice.
- McManus, Chamberlain, Loo, Rankin, Riley and Brunswick (2010, Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts) found that drawing ability among art students related to accuracy in copying simple angles and proportions (Cain's "house" task) and to visual memory. Poor drawers did worse on immediate and delayed recall of the Rey-Osterrieth complex figure. A follow-up on Cain's house task, also found by search, reports that the ability to copy simple angles and proportions relates to higher-level drawing ability.
- Shape constancy is a weak predictor. Cohen and Jones (2008) reported that drawing ability correlated with shape constancy. McManus and colleagues (2011) failed to replicate this twice, and their pooled analysis was non-significant. Ostrofsky, Kozbelt and Seidel (2012) found that both bottom-up factors (resisting size constancy) and top-down factors (choosing which lines matter in a limited line-tracing task) were associated with accuracy.
- Systematic biases follow perception. Ostrofsky, Kozbelt and Cohen (2015, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology) is titled as empirical support for the misperception hypothesis with two angle illusions. Search returned only its title and front matter. An Iowa State thesis reports that people drew 15 degree angles too large and 75 degree angles too small, consistent with coding angles into categories (near-vertical, near-horizontal, oblique). Ostrofsky and colleagues (2016) report that over 95 percent of participants in some studies drew the eyes too high on the head.
- Chamberlain, McManus, Brunswick, Rankin and Riley (2015, "Scratching the surface", Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts) studied 682 art students, 301 of whom did drawing and memory tasks. Drawing ability related to time spent drawing and to use of drawing techniques, with separate effects of copying and recalling a complex figure. "Surface" learners spent more time drawing, learned fewer techniques and reached lower skill. "Strategic" learners reached higher skill.
- Chamberlain, Kozbelt, Drake and Wagemans (2021, "Learning to see by learning to draw", Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts) followed 42 students through a five-month foundation drawing course. Drawing, mental rotation, disembedding figures and attention switching all improved, but gains on one task did not predict gains on another. The summary did not mention a control group, so the gains are observed change, not proven training effects.
- Chamberlain (2018, Current Directions in Psychological Science) frames drawing as an understudied domain of expertise and organises the research around visual attention and memory, personality and cognitive ability, and genes and environment.

What I could not find. Search turned up no randomised study testing whether negative-space or sighting instruction improves adult novices' accuracy. A 2022 Frontiers study gave 96 children and 24 adults a "draw the space around the objects" instruction and reports that it shifts attention toward space, but the search snippet gave no accuracy outcome. A University of Waterloo polygon analysis of drawings found experts more accurate on every error dimension. In that study people were more accurate on proportion and position in the positive shapes but more accurate on orientation in the negative shapes.

What this means for exercise design.
- Train looking and checking, not just the hand. The only controlled decomposition (Cohen and Bennett) puts motor control last. Line-control drills still matter for line quality and confidence, but they should not be sold as the fix for wrong proportions.
- Teach the expert looking pattern directly: short segments, frequent glances, one small area at a time (Cohen 2005; Tchalenko 2009). This can go in exercise wording and in how the reference viewer behaves. The reference should sit where a glance reaches it, and the phone beside the paper suits this.
- Angle and proportion copying is the measurable core (McManus 2010; Cain's house work). Drills should ask for specific angles and ratios and then check them against the reference with numbers.
- Expect known biases and name them: angles drift toward horizontal or vertical (Iowa State thesis), eyes are placed too high (Ostrofsky 2016). These belong in `common_mistakes` and in the overlay summary.
- Train checking of one's own drawing: flip, step back, predict where the errors are before overlaying. The evidence that perceiving one's own drawing matters is mixed (Cohen and Bennett against, Perdreau and Cavanagh for), but checking is cheap and the overlay gives ground truth.
- Measuring and sighting: standard in every syllabus the earlier pass read (Clark, SJSU, YSU, FUA, NWACC; see `data/sources/03` and `09`) and consistent with the angle and proportion findings, but untested in a controlled study that I found.
- Negative space: plausible as a way to redirect attention (Frontiers 2022) and as a second check on orientation (Waterloo), with no accuracy trial found.
- Blind contour: Tchalenko and Miall show that shape can be produced from eye-to-hand mapping without watching the hand, which fits blind contour as a looking exercise. I found no study showing it improves later sighted accuracy. Use it as a warm-up for looking, not as an accuracy method.
- Sight-size: an atelier method (the Florence Academy uses it for Bargue copies; `data/sources/09`). It removes the rescaling step and puts subject and drawing side by side at the same size. I found no controlled evidence. A phone-sized drawing beside the phone is close to sight-size, so the app can offer "draw at screen size" early on.
- Upside-down drawing: drop it as an accuracy method. Cohen and Earls (2010) found that inversion did not improve accuracy and hurt spatial details. Ostrofsky and colleagues (2016) found that inverted faces were drawn less accurately for long-range relations. Day and Davidenko (2018) found upright faces drawn more accurately, and Viviani and Bruno (2017, as summarised) found no gain.
- Practice volume is not enough on its own. Chamberlain 2015 found that drawing more without picking up techniques went with lower skill. Each session needs one named technique and a check.
- Gains are domain-specific (Chamberlain 2021). A phone game of estimating angles may not transfer to drawing. Keep measurement inside drawing tasks.

### A2. The "right brain" claim and Edwards's exercises

- The lateralisation story does not hold as a description of people. Nielsen and colleagues (2013, PLOS ONE) analysed resting-state scans from 1,011 people aged 7 to 29. Lateralised hubs exist, but people's strongly left- and right-lateralised connections did not covary, so the data give no support to a whole-brain "left-brained" or "right-brained" trait. Corballis (2014, PLoS Biology) reviews real hemispheric asymmetries and warns against exaggerated left-right polarity.
- Drawing skill itself does not map onto "the right side". Chamberlain and colleagues (2014, NeuroImage), a voxel-based morphometry study of 44 people titled "Drawing on the right side of the brain", linked observational drawing ability to grey matter in the left anterior cerebellum and right medial frontal gyrus, which the authors associate with fine motor control and procedural memory. Artistic training was linked to the right precuneus (visual imagery). The study is small and correlational. The search summary notes the title nods to Edwards rather than endorsing her theory.
- Edwards's own framing: her official site (drawright.com, theory page) says she named the modes "L-mode" and "R-mode" no matter where they sit in an individual brain. I could not confirm how later editions handle the criticism.
- The exercises: the inversion exercise is not supported (A1). Contour, negative space and sighting are standard syllabus content, but I found no controlled test of them. Search found no peer-reviewed pre/post evaluation of Edwards's course, only mention of a PhD thesis on her method and her selected before/after self-portraits from five-day workshops.
- Recommendation: use the exercises that match the accuracy evidence (sighting, negative space as a check, contour as a looking drill). Do not put brain-hemisphere language in the app.

### A3. Deliberate practice

- Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Romer (1993, Psychological Review) defined deliberate practice as individualised practice designed by a qualified teacher specifically to improve performance. The teacher-designed element is central. (Definition as given in the Macnamara and Maitra replication and other search results. I did not see the paper's full criteria list.)
- Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald (2014, Psychological Science) pooled 157 effect sizes from more than 11,000 participants. Deliberate practice explained about 26 percent of performance variance in games, 21 percent in music, 18 percent in sports, 4 percent in education and under 1 percent in professions. Ericsson's group disputes the broader definition they used.
- Macnamara and Maitra (2019, Royal Society Open Science) ran a double-blind replication of the violin study with 39 violinists. The core finding, that accumulated deliberate practice tracked each skill level, did not replicate: the effect was substantial but much smaller than the original. Teacher-designed practice was rated less relevant than practice alone. The sample was small.
- Drawing-specific: Chamberlain 2015 (A1) found that time spent drawing relates to skill, but how one practises matters. Strategic learners who picked up techniques did better than surface learners who drew more.
- What it means for a beginner: practice explains part of the variance, not most of it, so the app should promise improvement, not mastery on a schedule. The features of deliberate practice that do have support (a specific goal, attention, a check against a standard, repetition with correction) should be built into every drill. The app plays the "teacher-designed" role by choosing the next drill from logged errors.

### A4. Spacing, retrieval, interleaving and desirable difficulties

General learning science.
- Dunlosky and colleagues (2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest) rated practice testing and distributed practice as high utility. Interleaved practice was moderate because the evidence was still limited. Rereading, highlighting and summarising were low.
- Roediger and Karpicke (2006, Psychological Science): restudying won on a 5-minute test, but prior testing gave substantially better retention at one week, even though restudying made students more confident.
- Bjork's "desirable difficulties" (Bjork lab papers found by search): spacing, interleaving, varied conditions, intermittent rather than continuous feedback, and tests as learning events slow acquisition but improve later retention and transfer. The same paper stresses that many, perhaps most, difficulties are not desirable. The earlier curriculum pass read the Bjork and Bjork (2011) chapter as saying a difficulty only helps if the learner has the background to meet it (`data/sources/09`).
- Kornell and Bjork (2008, Psychological Science): people learned painters' styles better when paintings by different artists were interleaved than when they were blocked by artist, yet rated blocking as more effective. Later work attributes the benefit to "discriminative contrast" between categories. One exact replication of Experiment 2 was run in 2014; I could not see its result.
- Soderstrom and Bjork (2015, Perspectives on Psychological Science) review the gap between performance during practice and learning (found by search; I saw no detail).

Motor skills.
- Lee and Genovese (1988, Research Quarterly for Exercise and Sport): when trials are massed (short inter-trial intervals), both performance and learning measured by retention are depressed.
- Donovan and Radosevich (1999, Journal of Applied Psychology) pooled 63 studies and 112 effect sizes and found an overall effect of 0.46 favouring spaced over massed practice. Task type and inter-trial interval moderated it, and many studies found no effect.
- Shea, Lai, Black and Park (2000, Human Movement Science) is titled "Spacing practice sessions across days benefits the learning of motor skills". Search returned only the citation.
- Contextual interference (random or interleaved versus blocked practice of several skills): it usually lowers performance during practice but improves retention and transfer. Brady (2004) found larger effects in lab research (0.57) than applied research (0.19), and in adults (0.50) than in children (0.10). Czyz and colleagues (2024, Frontiers in Psychology) report a medium effect in adults (SMD 0.54) and a negligible one in young participants (0.12). A 2025 commentary raises concerns about study quality and publication bias.
- Kantak and Winstein (2012, Behavioural Brain Research): delayed retention or transfer is a better index of motor learning than end-of-practice performance. Conditions that make practice harder can lower practice performance while raising long-term retention.

Drawing. I found no study of spacing, retrieval or interleaving in drawing training.

What a spaced revisit of a drawing module should be (inference from the above):
- A retrieval attempt, not a rewatch. With the lesson closed, draw the module's key construction or exercise cold, then check it against the reference and the `done_when` items (testing effect; Dunlosky's low rating of rereading).
- Spaced across days, at intervals that grow when he passes and shrink when he does not (distributed practice; Shea 2000 title; Donovan and Radosevich). There is no drawing-specific evidence for exact intervals, so treat them as defaults to tune.
- Varied: revisit with a new subject that needs the same skill (a different cylindrical object, a new pose), because varied conditions and transfer tests are the point (Bjork; Kantak and Winstein).
- Judged on the delayed attempt. A module should not count as secure on a same-session drawing.
- Interleaved in short review blocks once he can do the exercise at all. Keep blocked practice for the first sessions of a new skill, since difficulty only helps with enough background (Bjork as read by the earlier pass) and contextual-interference benefits are smaller in applied settings (Brady).

### A5. Feedback, self-assessment and comparison

What kind of feedback helps.
- Hattie and Timperley (2007, Review of Educational Research) describe feedback at four levels: task, process, self-regulation and self (praise of the person). Task, process and self-regulation feedback help learning more than self-level feedback. The earlier pass recorded from the SAGE abstract and tables that praise averaged an effect size of 0.14, and that feedback built on change from the previous attempt did better than feedback without that comparison (`data/sources/09`).
- Kluger and DeNisi (1996, Psychological Bulletin) pooled 607 effect sizes (23,663 observations). Feedback raised performance on average (d = 0.41), but more than a third of feedback interventions lowered it. Effects weakened as feedback drew attention away from the task and toward the self.
- Motor learning: the guidance hypothesis (Salmoni, Schmidt and Walter, 1984) says feedback on every trial can create dependence and over-correction. The evidence is mixed: Wulf and Shea (2004) found little sign that feedback after every trial causes dependence. Learner-controlled feedback has helped in many studies. In Chiviacowsky and Wulf (2002) learners asked for feedback mostly after trials they thought were good, and in their 2005 study deciding after the trial gave better transfer than deciding before.

Self-assessment by novices.
- Agreement between students' self-ratings and teacher ratings tends to be only weakly positive (Brown and Harris 2013, as cited in a later review). In a 2025 study of 112 students (Pinedo, Panadero and colleagues), higher achievers underestimated and lower achievers overestimated. Rubrics improved absolute accuracy and reduced bias in one 2022 experiment with 93 students, while a separate review found that rubrics alone did little without feedback.
- The "Dunning-Kruger effect" is contested. Gignac and Zajenkowski (2020) found much less support with better statistical methods. Dunkel and colleagues (2023) argue a small effect may survive. A 2022 Frontiers paper shows that part of the pattern comes from bounded scales, since the lowest scorers can only err upward. Design for the robust finding (self-ratings are noisy and lower performers lean optimistic), not for the meme.

Comparison overlays and before/after.
- Comparison can mislead. Fiorella, Jaeger, Capobianco and Burnett (2024, Contemporary Educational Psychology) randomly assigned 116 students who had drawn the circulatory system either to compare their drawings with polished instructional visuals or to restudy text. Comparing made students underconfident about their drawing's quality and overconfident about future performance, because they compared surface style rather than content. This was a science-learning task, not observational drawing, but the mechanism applies to comparing against polished art.
- Overlay feedback during the task can become a crutch. In ArtKrit (UIST 2025), overlaying ground-truth contours led less experienced participants to trace over them. In a motor-control study on arXiv (2306.07012), a visual overlay improved performance during the task, but the learning gain was lower than with language corrections.
- Measured feedback on specific features helps novices in small studies. A SIGGRAPH 2025 paper (HKUST Guangzhou) built an interface that guides novices using professional artists' gaze patterns and found significantly more accurate shapes, but the training test had five beginners. A portrait-drawing learning assistant (MDPI Computers, 2024) reports that feedback on specific facial features from a normalised cross-correlation measure improved novice portrait accuracy. iCanDraw (Dixon, Prasad and Hammond, CHI 2010) gave corrective feedback on face drawings and produced nine design principles. I could not see its outcome data.
- Before/after galleries: I found no study. The earlier pass's sources (Will Kemp, Veronica's Art, Adventures with Art, Clip Studio; `data/sources/01`) recommend flipping, stepping away and overlaying to see errors that habituation hides. These are practitioner sources.

Implications: give task-level feedback tied to named checks, on his request rather than after every drawing. Show overlays after drawing, not during. Turn overlays into measured statements about specific landmarks rather than an impression of overall likeness. Compare him with the reference and with his own earlier attempts, not with polished exemplars.

### A6. Memory drawing and drawing from imagination

- Lecoq de Boisbaudran taught memory drawing in Paris. His students studied a painting at the Louvre and reproduced it from memory in the studio, starting with very simple subjects such as the outline of a nose and progressing by easy stages. Pupils named in the English translation's preface include Fantin-Latour, Legros, Cazin, Lhermitte, Tissot and Rodin, who later said most of what he was taught "is assuredly in me still" (Wikipedia and the translation's front matter, via search). The book (L. D. Luard translation, 1911) is public domain on the Internet Archive (earlier pass, `data/sources/03`).
- Sight-Size.com's abridgement of Richard Lack's 1990 article treats memory work as a supplement to observation, not a replacement (earlier pass, `data/sources/03`).
- Evidence that visual memory goes with drawing skill: recall of a complex figure predicts drawing ability (McManus 2010; Chamberlain 2015), and experts remember both the original and their drawing better (Perdreau and Cavanagh 2015). These are correlations. I found no controlled study showing that memory-drawing training improves drawing. A later project page found similar recognition memory in artists and non-artists but better recall in artists.
- Drawing from imagination: the earlier pass found Peter Han's "Training yourself to draw from imagination" on Proko and Oleander Studios' description of a visual library (collect references by subject, study from life, test recall without reference, vary viewpoints) (`data/sources/07`, `02`). These are practitioner sources. I could not search for wider consensus.
- Implication: memory drawing doubles as the retrieval step in A4, which makes it the best-supported "light retrieval" the user asked for. Keep it short and always follow it with a check against the source, as the `obs-visual-memory` module already does.

- Critique communities. r/learnart's rules ask for clear straight-on photos, real effort and constructive comments with examples. r/ArtCrit has more than 127,000 members and tags requests Beginner, Intermediate or Skilled. Both were read through mirrors. (`data/sources/01`.)
- Research systems (2010 to 2026). iCanDraw (CHI 2010) gave corrective feedback on face drawings from a photo. The portrait drawing learning assistant (MDPI 2024) scores facial features. The SIGGRAPH 2025 gaze-guided assistant improved novice shape accuracy in a small study. ArtKrit (UIST 2025 best paper) scaffolds composition, value and colour study from a reference for digital artists and was found to encourage reflection while working. Its longitudinal deployment (arXiv 2604.26935, DIS 2026) reports that it encouraged fundamentals practice, with the rule-of-thirds grid toggle among the most-used features. All of these work on a digital canvas, not a photo of paper.
- General-purpose vision models as critics: I found no peer-reviewed study of how accurately a multimodal model critiques drawings. The nearest evidence is a 2024 Georgia Tech evaluation of GPT-4 on visualisation literacy. It handled trends and extremes but struggled with precise value retrieval, could not reliably tell chart colours apart, and sometimes hallucinated or answered inconsistently.

### Patterns (inferences from the material above, not survey results)

- Free teaching is plentiful. Free, timely critique of your own drawing is scarce, and providers monetise it: Proko's critiques and projects are mostly premium, Line of Action and Drawabox centre their communities on submission. The free forums sampled were slow.
- Timed-reference tools are mature and free, but none of their feature lists, as recorded by the earlier pass, mention logging the drawing, checking it against a standard, or deciding the next drill.
- Courses teach then assign. None of the providers covered schedules review of earlier skills. I saw no spaced revisit in any product read, though this is absence in what was read, not proof.
- Measured accuracy feedback exists only in research prototypes on digital canvases. Nothing covered here measures a photographed paper drawing against its reference.

### Gaps no covered product fills

1. A single loop on paper: today's drill, timed reference, photo log, check against the module's standard, next step.
2. Spaced, varied revisits of earlier modules with retrieval drawing.
3. Objective accuracy numbers for a photographed paper drawing, such as landmark error and ratio error, tracked over time.
4. Critique grounded in the specific exercise's standard rather than general taste.

What makes beginners stick or quit: not researched. Reviews, Reddit threads and app store data were all out of reach, so I make no claims here. This should be the first job of a follow-up pass.

## C. Recommendations for this app

Evidence labels: "supported" means at least one source in Part A points this way. "Design choice" means a reasonable default with no direct evidence; make these tunable.

### C1. Session structure and timer defaults

A 30-minute session (the 3-hour template's weekday slot) as the default shape:
1. Warm-up, 3 to 5 minutes. Gesture: 8 x 30 s by default, or lines and ellipses for construction phases. Design choice; the durations follow atelier and tool conventions (A8).
2. Retrieval, 3 minutes. One due revisit (C3), drawn from memory with the lesson hidden, then a reveal of the reference and two or three taps on its checks. Supported (A4, A6).
3. Focus drill, 15 to 20 minutes. One module exercise with one named technique shown on screen ("this session: measure three angles before committing"). Supported (Chamberlain 2015 on technique use; deliberate practice, A3).
4. Check, 3 to 5 minutes. Flip, predict errors, overlay, then a one-line note (C4, C5). Supported (A5).
5. Stop on time. The app shows "next time" (the drill chosen from today's errors) and nothing else. Design choice, consistent with `found-practice-method`.

Timer details:
- Gesture ladder presets: "Warm-up" (8 x 30 s), "Short" (10 x 30 s, 5 x 1 min) and "Full" (the module's 10 x 30 s, 6 x 1 min, 4 x 2 min, 2 x 5 min). A 5-second gap between poses and a sound or haptic at 5 seconds left. Space pauses (already in the brief).
- For observation drills, show a quiet reminder of the expert looking pattern in the reference viewer ("short segments, look often"), not a timer cue. Supported in content (Cohen 2005; Tchalenko 2009). An optional "look up" metronome would be an experiment; only build it if he asks.
- A 10-minute "tired day" session (warm-up plus one revisit) so a session never has to be skipped for lack of time. Design choice.

### C2. Sequencing new material and review

- Every session after the first week contains at least one revisit. Rough split while a module is active: about 70 percent current module, 30 percent review. Shift toward more review in the week before a phase benchmark. Design choice built on A4.
- Block the first two or three sessions of a new exercise, then interleave it with earlier ones in the review block. Supported in direction (Bjork on background; contextual interference smaller in applied and novice settings, A4).
- Count a module as done only when its outcome checks pass on a later day than first practice, not in the session where it was learned. Supported (Kantak and Winstein; Roediger and Karpicke).
- Phase benchmarks stay as written, run when the group is done, and the overlay benchmark is repeated a week later as the curriculum already specifies.

### C3. What "spaced revisit" means for a module

- Unit of review: each module defines one "revisit task", a 3 to 10 minute cold version of its core exercise drawn without the lesson. Examples: a cylinder at a new angle from memory, a 2-minute pose redrawn from yesterday, a three-object block-in from a new photo. Add a `revisit` field per module, with its own short `done_when` subset. Supported in form (testing effect; variation).
- Schedule: first revisit after 2 days, then about 1 week, 3 weeks and 2 months. Passing roughly doubles the next interval; "not yet" resets it to 2 to 3 days and marks the module weak. The intervals are a design choice; the curriculum's own 1 day, 3 days, 1 week ladder in `found-practice-method` is a reasonable short-term start.
- Variation by default: each revisit uses a different subject or reference from the library than the last attempt.
- Weak-module detection uses three signals: checks marked "not yet" or "partly" on the last two attempts, overlay error above the benchmark tolerance, and no attempt in more than twice the current interval.
- Never pile up. Show at most one or two revisits per session, and if the queue grows, drop the oldest low-priority items silently rather than showing a backlog count. Design choice; a growing review backlog is the spaced-repetition version of a broken streak.

### C4. Drawing log and self-rating

What to ask, in this order, in under a minute:
1. Photo, auto-cropped to the paper using its four corners, so the overlay can be rectified. Design choice.
2. Predict before seeing anything: "Where is it most off?" Tap up to three spots on the photo and pick a direction (too wide or narrow, too tall or short, angle too steep or too flat). This trains perception of his own drawing and creates a calibration record. Supported in spirit (Perdreau and Cavanagh; Ostrofsky 2015 on own-drawing perception), new as a feature.
3. Overlay and numbers (C5).
4. Per-check rating: each of the module's outcome `done_when` items as "yes", "partly", "not yet" or "can't tell". No global 1 to 5 score, no "how good is it?". Supported (task-level feedback; Kluger and DeNisi on self-focus).
5. One-line note, optional, prefilled with the measured error ("verticals 8 percent long").

How to limit novice overconfidence:
- Split `done_when` into three kinds and handle each differently: process counts ("logged 12 sessions") are computed from the log, never self-rated; measurable checks (proportion, landmark position) are decided by overlay numbers; judgment checks (line confidence, gesture reads) are self-rated, with the optional AI second opinion. Design choice, grounded in the weak correlation between self- and expert ratings (A5).
- Show one anchor pair per judgment check: a "meets" and a "not yet" example on a similar task. Use his own earlier drawings once they exist, and plain diagrams before that, not polished artist exemplars, to avoid surface-style comparison (Fiorella 2024). Rubric evidence is mixed (A5), so anchors support judgment rather than replace measurement.
- Show calibration, not grades: over time, how often his predicted error spots matched the overlay, and how often his "yes" matched the measured result. Rising calibration is a real, sourced skill (experts perceive their own drawings better) and it is encouraging without being a streak.
- Ask for an optional re-rating the next morning (fresh eyes) and keep both ratings. Design choice from practitioner advice (`data/sources/01`).
- Rewrite the three streak-worded `done_when` items: "kept a weekly schedule for three weeks in a row" could become "completed at least 8 of your planned sessions in the last four weeks", and similarly for `des-prompt-challenges` and `obs-negative-space`.

### C5. Comparison tools

- Overlay, after drawing only. The reference is never overlaid on the live paper view; that invites tracing (ArtKrit) and helps performance more than learning (arXiv 2306.07012). Supported.
- Alignment by landmarks: he taps 4 to 10 matching points on the reference and on the rectified photo. The app fits a similarity transform (scale, rotation, translation, no shear) and reports residual error per point as a percentage of image height, plus height-to-width ratio error per object. This matches the benchmark's existing 5 and 10 percent tolerances and works on paper photos without any model. Design choice; deterministic, cheap, and it turns the overlay into the measured, feature-specific feedback the small studies found useful (A5).
- Error summary in words, naming the direction and the known bias where it applies: "angles flatter than the reference on 4 of 5", "eyes placed 6 percent high". Supported (Iowa State thesis; Ostrofsky 2016; Hattie and Timperley on task-level feedback).
- Views: 50 percent opacity overlay with slider, a blink toggle, side by side at the same size (sight-size style), and flip for both images.
- Before/after per module: pair the first logged attempt with the latest attempt of the same exercise, ideally the same or a similar reference, with the measured error under each. Show the overlay-error trend for accuracy modules as a small line, not a score. Compare only like with like (same exercise and similar time limit), or the comparison misleads. Design choice.

### C6. Optional AI critique

How it should work:
- On request only, after he has locked in his own prediction and per-check ratings. This keeps the AI from anchoring his self-assessment and lets the app measure agreement. Supported in direction (learner-controlled feedback; guidance hypothesis, A5).
- Grounded input: the module's exercise text, its outcome `done_when` items, its `common_mistakes`, the benchmark `pass_signs` when it is a benchmark, the reference image, the rectified photo, the overlay numbers, and his own ratings. The prompt forbids judging anything outside those checks.
- Constrained output, as structured JSON the app renders: for each check, "meets", "not yet" or "can't tell from photo", with the specific location or feature it is based on; then the single most important fix, one drill to address it chosen from the curriculum's own practice items, and an optional note where the AI disagrees with his rating. No praise of the person, no overall grade, no style advice. Supported (Hattie and Timperley; Kluger and DeNisi).
- Division of labour: geometry (proportion, placement, angles) comes from the deterministic overlay, never from the model. The model handles qualitative checks such as line confidence, whether a gesture reads, and value grouping. Supported by the absence of drawing-critique validation and the documented weakness of GPT-4 at precise visual value reading and its inconsistency (Georgia Tech 2024).

Risks and mitigations:
- Hallucinated or confident errors: require "can't tell", show the evidence location for each verdict, and let him mark a verdict as wrong. Log those marks to judge whether the feature earns its place.
- Inconsistency between runs: fixed prompt and rubric, one critique per drawing by default, and no "regenerate until nicer" button. Design choice.
- Over-reliance: suggest AI critique at benchmarks and at most once or twice a week otherwise, and keep the default flow working fully without it. Supported in direction (guidance hypothesis; overlay dependence).
- Photo artefacts (glare, keystone, paper texture) can be misread as drawing errors: rectify first and show the AI the cropped image.
- Privacy and cost: the image goes to the model provider through his own Claude Code or Codex CLI. Say so once, plainly, and store critiques locally with the drawing.
- Do not generate a "corrected" version of his drawing to look at or trace. It invites the same tracing and surface-comparison problems (ArtKrit; Fiorella 2024), and an image model's correction is not a measured truth.

### C7. Keeping a beginner going without streaks

- A record, not a streak: a quiet weekly view of sessions done against plan, with no counter, no "broken" state and no catch-up stacking. Missed sessions are dropped, as the templates already say.
- Make progress visible in ways that are true: module before/after pairs, overlay error trending down, calibration trending up. These are task-level and fit the feedback evidence (A5).
- One clear next step on Today, and nothing to browse unless he wants to.
- Choice without a menu: Today offers one recommended session, with a single "swap" that gives two alternatives (a play session, or a different revisit). Supported indirectly by learner-control findings (A5, A7).
- Protect play: keep the weekly free-drawing session from the templates, and let a "Surprise me" prompt count as a real session in the record. Whether a 50 percent free-drawing share (Drawabox) is right is unverified; one play session in four, as in the 3-hour template, is a sensible floor.
- A rest valve: after two missed planned sessions, Today offers the 10-minute session and a play prompt, not a warning.
- No badges, levels or praise copy (Kluger and DeNisi on attention to the self).
